\begin{abstract}
Minibatch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) weights each group of a
population by how often it appears in the data, not by how much it
matters, so on imbalanced data it minimizes a surrogate risk that favors
frequent groups, however long it runs. We show that reweighting the groups
inside each batch reaches only a polytope of objectives fixed by the
sampler, which contains the target exactly when a masked optimal transport
problem between the law of batch compositions and the target is feasible.
The resulting method, \avot{}, reweights every batch with a Sinkhorn plan
computed once and, when the target is reachable, optimizes it at the
$O(1/\sqrt{T})$ rate of SGD. Otherwise, Sinkhorn returns the max--min fair
Kullback--Leibler projection of the target, and a
mean--conditional-value-at-risk (CVaR) layer certifies the target risk
through its distributionally robust form. On Adult and IMDb-Wiki, \avot{}
closes $52$--$64\%$ of the gap between group-blind averaging and
full-access SGD, and it wins wherever correcting the weighting saves more
risk than its larger weights add in gradient noise.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Group fairness, imbalanced learning, stochastic gradient descent, masked
optimal transport, conditional value at risk.
\end{keywords}

% =====================================================================
\vspace{-4pt}\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

Machine learning fits a model by minimizing its average loss over the
population it will serve, at scale by stochastic gradient descent (SGD),
one small random batch at a time \cite{nemirovski2009,bottou2018optimization}.
SGD thus follows the population loss in the proportions in which the data
arrive, and those proportions are rarely the ones that matter.
Populations consist of groups, such as demographic categories, speakers
or identities, whose share of the data differs from their importance:
face-analysis systems err most on the intersections least present in
their training data \cite{buolamwini2018gender}, speech recognizers make
markedly more errors for Black than for white speakers
\cite{koenecke2020speech}, and imbalanced regression errs most where labels
are scarce \cite{yang2021dir}. Imbalance is the norm
\cite{he2009imbalanced}, and SGD inherits it: each group steers the updates
in proportion to its frequency.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:motivation}, two groups, one nine times as frequent as
the other, follow different trends, and the learner weighs them equally.
Uniform SGD fits the frequent group and leaves the rare one with nearly
twenty times its error, upsampling the rare group inside each batch
narrows the gap, and \avot{} recovers the target line, with equal error
on both groups.

Existing remedies act on the sampler, the loss or the objective.
Resampling \cite{he2009imbalanced,chawla2002smote} and balanced sampling
\cite{idrissi2022simple} need control of the sampler, and down-sampling
wastes data. Reweighting by class \cite{cui2019classbalanced} or label
\cite{yang2021dir} frequency and margin or logit adjustment
\cite{cao2019ldam,menon2021logit} favor rare classes, though importance
weights fade on separable data \cite{byrd2019importance}, and importance
sampling \cite{needell2014sgd,katharopoulos2018not} only reduces variance.
Group distributionally robust optimization (DRO) \cite{sagawa2020dro} and
its label-free variants \cite{hashimoto2018repeated,liu2021jtt}, CVaR and
DRO objectives \cite{namkoong2017variance,levy2020large,curi2020adaptive}, and
constrained methods
\cite{hardt2016equality,agarwal2018reductions,cotter2019twoplayer,chamon2020pacc,chamon2023nonconvex}
change the objective itself. In streaming, sharded or access-restricted
pipelines, however, batches arrive as they come, and the only lever left
is how the groups present in each batch are weighted.

Upsampling, the natural choice, sets each group's weight from its own
frequency and renormalizes the batch, so the weight the frequent group
collects when alone is never returned to the rare group. We instead
choose the weights of all batch compositions jointly, as a transport plan
from how often each combination of groups appears to how much each group
matters. This carries over \textsf{FedAVOT} \cite{rahimi2025fedavot} and
\textsf{FedeRage} \cite{rahimi2026federage}, which correct and hedge the
analogous mismatch between client participation and client importance in
federated learning. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt\parskip0pt\topsep2pt
\item \emph{What reweighting can reach.} SGD, and every normalized rule
that reweights the groups of a batch, optimizes a surrogate whose
weighting lies in a polytope fixed by the sampler; the target lies in it
exactly when a Hall-type coverage condition holds, checked by one
max-flow before training.
\item \emph{Exact alignment.} In feasible cases, \avot{} is unbiased for the
target and converges at the $O(1/\sqrt T)$ rate of SGD whatever the batch composition.
\item \emph{Certified robustness beyond coverage.} Otherwise, Sinkhorn
returns the max--min fair Kullback--Leibler projection of the target onto
the reachable set, and a mean--CVaR layer certifies the target risk at the
smallest price any normalized rule admits. One theorem covers both regimes
at the $O(1/\sqrt T)$ rate, inflated by $\kappa$ under CVaR.
\item \emph{Evidence.} On Adult and IMDb-Wiki, \avot{} closes
$52$--$64\%$ of the gap between group-blind averaging and full-access SGD
and cuts the cross-entropy of race Other (under $1\%$ of the records) from
$0.119$ to $0.073$. It wins while the alignment gain exceeds the noise of
its larger weights, though its weighting is always closer to $p$, and
CVaR buys a certificate rather than a lower risk.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{-6pt}\section{Problem Formulation}
\vspace{-8pt}
\label{sec:setup}
% =====================================================================

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Group-fair SGD by masked transport (\avot{})}
\label{alg:gavot}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $p$, $(q,\{A_j\},E)$, stepsizes $\eta_t$, horizon $T$,
$\alpha\in(0,1]$, $\gamma\in[0,1]$
\State $\Gamma\leftarrow$ Sinkhorn$(\mathbf 1_E;p,q)$,
$\ W\leftarrow\Gamma\,\mathrm{diag}(q)^{-1}$ \Comment{offline}
\State $\theta^0\in\mathcal{C}$, $\ \tau^0\in[0,M]$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \State draw $B^t$; find $j$ with $A_j=A^t$; get $g^t_i,\hat f^t_i$, $i\in A^t$
  \State $c^t_i\leftarrow\mathbf{1}\{\hat f^t_i\ge\tau^{t-1}\}$, $\ \omega^t_i\leftarrow W[i,j]\big(1-\gamma+\tfrac{\gamma}{\alpha}c^t_i\big)$
  \State $\theta^{t}\leftarrow\Proj\big(\theta^{t-1}-\eta_t\sum_{i\in A^t}\omega^t_i\,g^t_i\big)$
  \State $\tau^{t}\leftarrow\Pi_{[0,M]}\big(\tau^{t-1}-\eta_t\gamma\big(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i\in A^t}W[i,j]c^t_i\big)\big)$
\EndFor
\Ensure $(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\theta^t,\tau^t)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
Let $\mathcal{X}\subseteq\R^{d_x}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ be the input and
output spaces, and let $\Nn{N}$ index the groups over which fairness is
required. The training set $\mathcal{S}$ holds $n$ triples $(x,y,z)$ with
group label $z\in\Nn{N}$. Group $i$ has $n_i$ examples, empirical law
$\mathcal{D}_i$ and \emph{prevalence} $\pi_i\triangleq n_i/n$. A model
$m(\cdot\,;\theta)$ with parameter $\theta$ in a convex compact set
$\mathcal{C}\subseteq\R^{d}$ incurs a loss $\ell(m(x;\theta),y)\in[0,M]$,
and group $i$ incurs the risk
$f_i(\theta)\triangleq\E_{\mathcal{D}_i}[\ell(m(X;\theta),Y)]$. Each
weighting $w\in\simplex{N}$ of the groups defines the risk
$F_w\triangleq\sum_iw_if_i$ with a minimizer
$\theta^\star_w\in\argmin_{\mathcal{C}}F_w$. Three weightings matter in
what follows. The learner declares the first, the sampler imposes the
second, and a reweighting rule achieves the third.

\noindent\textbf{Target.} The learner declares a \emph{target}
(importance) distribution $p\in\simplex{N}$, which may be uniform,
inverse-prevalence, or set by a fairness specification
\cite{mohri2019agnostic,li2020fair}, and seeks
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\ F_p(\theta)=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_if_i(\theta).
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
\vspace{-10pt}

\noindent\textbf{Availability.} SGD never sees $p$. It sees random
batches, and the groups present in them determine which objectives it can
optimize. At step $t$ the batch $B^t$ holds $b$ examples drawn i.i.d.\
uniformly from $\mathcal{S}$, independently across steps. The groups it
contains form the \emph{active set} $A^t\triangleq\{z_k:k\in B^t\}$, which
takes values in a family $\{A_j\}_{j\in\Nn{J}}$ with law
$q_j\triangleq\PP[A^t=A_j]$, the \emph{availability distribution}. The
\emph{mask} $E\triangleq\{(i,j):i\in A_j\}$ records which groups each
composition contains. The \emph{coverage}
$\bar q(I)\triangleq\PP[A^t\cap I\neq\emptyset]$ of a set
$I\subseteq\Nn{N}$ is the probability that a batch contains at least one
of its groups, and group $i$ is observed at rate
$r_i\triangleq\bar q(\{i\})$. With $\pi(I)\triangleq\sum_{i\in I}\pi_i$,
inclusion--exclusion gives
\begin{multline}
\PP[A^t\!=\!A]=\textstyle\sum_{A'\subseteq A}(-1)^{|A\setminus A'|}\pi(A')^b,
\\ \bar q(I)=1-(1-\pi(I))^b,
\label{eq:iidlaw}
\end{multline}
so $r_i=1-(1-\pi_i)^b$. The analysis uses the sampler only through $q$,
so it covers every sampler with i.i.d.\ active sets, including the one of
Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}, which draws $K$ groups per step.

\noindent\textbf{Group-weighting rules.} Within a batch, the only freedom
left is how to weight the groups present. Let $g^t_i$ and $\hat f^t_i$ be
the subgradient and the loss at $\theta^{t-1}$, averaged over the
$n_i(B^t)$ examples of group $i$ in $B^t$. SGD and each of its
corrections step as $\theta^t=\Proj(\theta^{t-1}-\eta_t\hat g^t)$ along
$\hat g^t=\sum_{i\in A^t}\omega^t_ig^t_i$, and they differ only in the
batch weights $\omega^t$. A \emph{group-weighting rule} chooses
$\omega^t\in\simplex{N}$ supported on $A^t$ and depending on $B^t$ only
through its labels $\mathcal{Z}^t\triangleq(z_k)_{k\in B^t}$. We call such
rules \emph{normalized}, since the weights of every batch sum to one.
When the weights depend only on $A^t$, the rule is a matrix $W$ whose
$j$-th column weights the batches of composition $A_j$, which is the
client-weighting setting of \cite{rahimi2025fedavot,rahimi2026federage}
with groups in place of clients. Uniform SGD uses
$\omega^t_i=n_i(B^t)/b$, group-blind averaging $\omega^t_i=1/|A^t|$, and
upsampling $\omega^t_i\propto p_i/r_i$ on $A^t$. The \emph{effective
weighting} of a rule is $\tilde p\triangleq\E[\omega^t]$, and the next
result shows that it is the weighting the rule optimizes.
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{proposition}[Reachable objectives]
\label{prop:surrogate}
If the losses are convex in $\theta$, the batches are independent across
steps, and, given $\mathcal{Z}^t$, the examples of each group $i\in A^t$
are i.i.d.\ draws from $\mathcal{D}_i$ (as for i.i.d.\ batches), then a
group-weighting rule is a stochastic subgradient method on $F_{\tilde p}$,
and $\tilde p$ lies in the \emph{reachable set}
\begin{equation}
\Reach\triangleq\big\{\Gamma\mathbf 1_J:\ \Gamma\in\R_+^{N\times J},\
\mathbf 1_N^\top\Gamma=q^\top,\ \mathrm{supp}(\Gamma)\subseteq E\big\};
\label{eq:reach}
\end{equation}
conversely, every point of $\Reach$ is the effective weighting of some
rule. Uniform SGD has $\tilde p=\pi$, and group-blind averaging the
survival weights $\tilde p_i=\sum_{j:\,i\in A_j}q_j/|A_j|$ of
\cite{rahimi2025agnostic,rahimi2026federage}.
\end{proposition}
\vspace{-8pt}
\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} Given $\mathcal{Z}^t$, $\omega^t$ is fixed and
$\E[g^t_i\mid\theta^{t-1},\mathcal{Z}^t]=s_i\in\partial f_i(\theta^{t-1})$,
so $\E[\hat g^t\mid\theta^{t-1}]=\sum_i\tilde p_is_i\in\partial F_{\tilde
p}(\theta^{t-1})$. $\Gamma[i,j]\triangleq q_j\E[\omega^t_i\mid A^t=A_j]$
meets \eqref{eq:reach} with $\Gamma\mathbf 1_J=\tilde p$, and any such
$\Gamma$ is realized by $\omega^t=\Gamma[\cdot,j]/q_j$ on
$\{A^t=A_j\}$.\hfill$\square$

Proposition~\ref{prop:surrogate} has two consequences. The first concerns
SGD itself. Its group counts are multinomial with parameters $(b,\pi)$,
so $\tilde p=\pi$ and
\begin{equation}
\E\big[\hat g^t\mid\theta^{t-1}\big]\in\partial F_\pi(\theta^{t-1}).
\label{eq:introbias}
\end{equation}
The sampler thus imposes the prevalence $\pi$. SGD is a stochastic
subgradient method on $F_\pi$ and converges to $\theta^\star_\pi$, not to
$\theta^\star_p$, for every horizon and stepsize, just as agnostic
federated averaging converges to the participation-weighted optimum
\cite{rahimi2025agnostic,rahimi2026federage}. The second concerns every
normalized rule. Some rule optimizes \eqref{eq:target} if and only if
$p\in\Reach$, that is, if and only if the availability $q$ can be
transported onto the target $p$ along the mask $E$. \textsf{FedAVOT}
\cite{rahimi2025fedavot} removes the analogous mismatch in federated
averaging by masked optimal transport, and \textsf{FedeRage}
\cite{rahimi2026federage} hedges it by CVaR. Section~\ref{sec:gavot}
carries both tools over to SGD.

\section{Group-Fair SGD by MOT and CVaR}
\vspace{-8pt}
\label{sec:gavot}\label{sec:cvar}% sec:cvar kept as an alias
% =====================================================================

By Proposition~\ref{prop:surrogate}, designing a normalized rule amounts
to choosing a plan $\Gamma$, and the rule then optimizes the row marginal
$\Gamma\mathbf 1_J$. \avot{} chooses the plan in two stages. Masked
transport selects the reachable weighting closest to $p$, which is $p$
itself when $p$ is reachable, and a mean--CVaR layer bounds the part of
the target that no plan reaches. One theorem covers both stages.

\noindent\textbf{Transport as reweighting.} A rule optimizes $F_p$ exactly
when its plan, whose entry $\Gamma[i,j]$ is the mass that composition
$A_j$ sends to group $i$, solves the \emph{masked optimal transport} (MOT)
problem of \textsf{FedAVOT} \cite{rahimi2025fedavot}, a transport problem
\cite{villani2009,peyre2019} without cost,
\begin{equation}
\Gamma\mathbf 1_J=p,\qquad \mathbf 1_N^\top\Gamma=q^\top,\qquad \Gamma\ge 0,
\qquad \mathrm{supp}(\Gamma)\subseteq E .
\label{eq:mot}
\end{equation}
The rule is then $\omega^t=W[\cdot,j(t)]$, where $A^t=A_{j(t)}$ and
$W\triangleq\Gamma\,\mathrm{diag}(q)^{-1}$. The column constraint makes the
weights of every batch sum to one, the mask assigns weight only to groups
present in the batch, and the row constraint makes the weight of group
$i$, averaged over batches, equal to $p_i$. In Fig.~\ref{fig:motivation},
a batch holds only the majority with probability $0.9^7\approx0.48$. The
plan gives the minority $0.96$ of every mixed batch and reaches
$(0.48+0.52\cdot0.04,\,0.52\cdot0.96)\approx p$. Upsampling gives it only
$0.66$ and reaches $(0.66,0.34)$, because it weights each group by its own
rate, so the weight the majority collects in batches without the minority
is never returned. A plan with the marginals of \eqref{eq:mot} exists only
if the batches cover the target well enough, and
Proposition~\ref{thm:feas} states this condition exactly.


\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{proposition}[Coverage, cf.\ {\cite[Thm.~1]{rahimi2025fedavot}}]
\label{thm:feas}
Problem \eqref{eq:mot} is feasible, i.e.\ $p\in\Reach$, if and only if
\vspace{-9pt}

\begin{equation}
\textstyle p(I)\triangleq\sum_{i\in I}p_i\;\le\;\bar q(I)
\qquad\forall\,I\subseteq\Nn{N}.
\label{eq:hall}
\end{equation}
%For i.i.d.\ batches, $I=\{i\}$ and $I=\Nn{N}\setminus\{i\}$ give
%$\pi_i^b\le p_i\le1-(1-\pi_i)^b$.
\end{proposition}
\vspace{-6pt}
\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} Max-flow/min-cut
\cite{hall1935,fordfulkerson1956} on $s\to i\to j\to t$ with capacities
$p_i$, $\infty$ on $E$, and $q_j$; the lower inequalities of
\cite[Thm.~1]{rahimi2025fedavot} are \eqref{eq:hall} for
$\Nn{N}\setminus I$.\hfill$\square$

Condition \eqref{eq:hall} requires that no set of groups carry more
importance than the probability that a batch contains one of them. Since
$\bar q(I)\to1$ as $b$ grows, infeasibility arises with small batches and
rare groups. Two quantities measure how far an instance is from
feasibility, the \emph{Hall ratio}
$\rho\triangleq\max_{I\neq\emptyset}p(I)/\bar q(I)\ge1$, which equals one
exactly on feasible instances, and the \emph{infeasible mass}
$\nu\triangleq\sum_{i:\,p_i>r_i}p_i$ of the groups whose importance
exceeds their observation rate.

\noindent\textbf{Sinkhorn and the KL projection.} \avot{} computes the
plan by Sinkhorn scaling of $\mathbf 1_E$ to the marginals $(p,q)$
\cite{sinkhorn1967,cuturi2013,peyre2019}, once and offline, at a cost of
$O(|E|)$ per sweep. Under \eqref{eq:hall}, the scaling converges to a
solution of \eqref{eq:mot}. When \eqref{eq:hall} fails, no solution
exists, yet the iterates taken after each column scaling still normalize
every batch, and their row marginals converge \cite{gietl2013ipfp}. The
next result identifies their limit.

\vspace{-8pt}
\begin{proposition}[Closest reachable weighting]
\label{prop:proj}
The row marginal $\hat p$ of the limit of the column-scaled Sinkhorn
iterates satisfies
(i) $\hat p=\argmin_{x\in\Reach}\KL(x\,\|\,p)$, the I-projection of $p$
onto the reachable set \cite{csiszar1975}, and each composition sends its
mass only to the groups it contains with the smallest ratio $\hat p_i/p_i$;
(ii) $\max_ip_i/\hat p_i=\min_{x\in\Reach}\max_ip_i/x_i=\rho$.
\end{proposition}
\vspace{-8pt}
\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} (i) The limit is identified in
\cite{gietl2013ipfp}, 
and the second claim is the optimality condition of the projection; it
involves $x$ only through $x_i/p_i$, so $\hat p$ also minimizes
$\KL(p\,\|\,x)$ and is the lexicographically optimal base of the
polymatroid $\bar q$ \cite{fujishige1980}. (ii) For $x\in\Reach$ and any
$I$, $\min_ix_i/p_i\le x(I)/p(I)\le\bar q(I)/p(I)$ by
Proposition~\ref{thm:feas}, so $\max_ip_i/x_i\ge\rho$. By (i), the groups
$I_0$ of smallest ratio receive all the mass of every composition
touching them, so $\hat p(I_0)=\bar q(I_0)$ and
$\max_ip_i/\hat p_i=p(I_0)/\bar q(I_0)\le\rho$.\hfill$\square$

Transport thus returns the reachable weighting closest to $p$ in
Kullback--Leibler divergence, in either direction. It is also max--min
fair, since no normalized rule gives the worst-served group a larger
fraction of its importance. Under \eqref{eq:hall} this weighting is $p$
itself, and transport alone optimizes the target.

\noindent\textbf{Controlling the unreachable part by CVaR.} When $\rho>1$,
the weighting $\hat p$ differs from $p$, and optimizing $F_{\hat p}$
controls the target only through
$F_p\le F_{\hat p}+\frac M2\|p-\hat p\|_1$. \textsf{FedeRage}
\cite{rahimi2026federage} hedges the same gap in federated averaging with
a mean--CVaR objective that is robust to weightings near the imposed one.
We center this objective at $\hat p$. For $w\in\simplex{N}$,
$\alpha\in(0,1]$, $\gamma\in[0,1]$ and
$\kappa\triangleq(1-\gamma)+\gamma/\alpha$ \cite{rahimi2026federage}, let
\vspace{-8pt}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\label{eq:meancvar}
F^{\alpha,\gamma}_w&\triangleq(1-\gamma)F_w+\gamma\,\CVaR^w_\alpha(f)
=\max\nolimits_{v\in\,\Uset(w)}F_v,\\
\Uset(w)&\triangleq\big\{v\in\simplex{N}:\ (1-\gamma)w\le v\le\kappa\,w\big\}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\CVaR^w_\alpha$ is taken over the group drawn from $w$. The
equality is the dual representation of CVaR \cite{shapiro2009lectures},
written in $v=(1-\gamma)w+\gamma u$ with $u\le w/\alpha$. Hence
$F^{\alpha,\gamma}_w$ bounds the risk of every weighting whose ratio to
$w$ lies in $[1-\gamma,\kappa]$, and in particular bounds $F_p$ whenever
$p\in\Uset(w)$.

To optimize $F^{\alpha,\gamma}_{\hat p}$ from batches, we place the risk
on the observed loss, as \textsf{FedeRage} does. Given $B^t$, let
$I^t\mid B^t\sim W[\cdot,j(t)]$ be a group of the batch and
$L\triangleq\hat f^t_{I^t}(\theta)$, so that $\E L=F_{\hat p}(\theta)$ and
$\E[L\mid I^t=i]=f_i(\theta)$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:gavot} never samples
$I^t$. Lines 5--7 average over it in closed form and take exact projected
stochastic subgradient steps on the jointly convex Rockafellar--Uryasev
form \cite{rockafellar2000cvar}
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}(\theta,\tau)\triangleq
\E\big[(1-\gamma)L+\gamma\tau+\tfrac{\gamma}{\alpha}(L-\tau)_+\big].
\label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
Jensen's inequality conditional on $I^t$ then gives, for all
$(\theta,\tau)$,
\begin{equation}
F_{\hat p}(\theta)\;\le\;F^{\alpha,\gamma}_{\hat p}(\theta)\;\le\;\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}(\theta,\tau),
\label{eq:sandwich}
\end{equation}
so the algorithm drives down an upper bound on the robust risk around
$\hat p$.

\noindent\textbf{Why transport first.} This bound has a price. A bound
$F_p\le F^{\alpha,\gamma}_w$ valid for every loss profile needs
$p\le\kappa w$, so $\kappa\ge\max_ip_i/w_i$, and $\kappa$ inflates the
rate in Theorem~\ref{thm:rate}. Hedging around the prevalence $\pi$ that
SGD imposes costs $\kappa\ge\max_ip_i/\pi_i$. Hedging around $\hat p$
costs only $\rho$, the least any normalized rule admits by
Proposition~\ref{prop:proj}(ii). Transport therefore makes the CVaR layer
as cheap as possible, and the next theorem quantifies both stages.

\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{theorem}[Convergence of \avot{}]
\label{thm:rate}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ have diameter $D$, let each $\ell(m(x;\cdot),y)$ be
convex on $\mathcal{C}$, let the batches be i.i.d.\ across steps with
$\E[\|g^t_i\|^2\mid\theta^{t-1},\mathcal{Z}^t]\le G^2$, let $\Gamma$ be the
Sinkhorn plan of Proposition~\ref{prop:proj}, with row marginal $\hat p$,
and let $(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)$ be the output of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:gavot}.\\
(i) \emph{Transport.} If $\gamma=0$ and $\eta_t\equiv D/(G\sqrt{T})$, then
$\E[\hat g^t\mid\theta^{t-1}]\in\partial F_{\hat p}(\theta^{t-1})$ and
$\E[\|\hat g^t\|^2\mid\theta^{t-1}]\le G^2$, independently of $|A^t|$,
$N$, $b$ and $r$, and
\begin{equation}
\E F_{\hat p}(\bar\theta_T)-F_{\hat p}(\theta^\star_{\hat p})\;\le\;DG/\sqrt{T},
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
where $\hat p=p$, i.e.\ the target itself is optimized, whenever
\eqref{eq:hall} holds.\\
(ii) \emph{Transport and CVaR.} Let $\gamma\in(0,1]$, $\alpha\in(0,1]$,
$C_0\triangleq\sqrt{(D^2+M^2)(G^2+1)}$, and
$\eta_t\equiv\sqrt{D^2+M^2}/(\kappa\sqrt{(G^2+1)T})$. Then
\vspace{-12pt}
\begin{equation*}
\E\,\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)
-\min\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}\times[0,M]}\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}\;\le\;\kappa\,C_0/\sqrt T,
\end{equation*}\vspace{-8pt}
\vspace{-8pt}

and if moreover $p\in\Uset(\hat p)$, i.e.\ $\kappa\ge\rho$ and
$1-\gamma\le\min_ip_i/\hat p_i$, then\vspace{-8pt}

\begin{equation}
\E F_p(\bar\theta_T)\;\le\;\min\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}\times[0,M]}\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}
+\kappa\,C_0/\sqrt{T}.
\label{eq:certificate}
\end{equation}\vspace{-8pt}

\end{theorem}\vspace{-12pt}

\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} (i) Conditionally on $\theta^{t-1}$, the
columns of $W$ sum to one and the rows of $\Gamma$ to $\hat p$, so
$\E\hat g^t=\sum_jq_j\sum_{i\in A_j}W[i,j]\,s_i=\sum_i\hat p_is_i$ with
$s_i\in\partial f_i(\theta^{t-1})$, and Jensen's inequality over
$W[\cdot,j]$ bounds the second moment by $G^2$. This is the
sample-to-model argument of \textsf{FedAVOT}
\cite[Lemma~1, Thm.~3]{rahimi2025fedavot} with one local step, closed by
the projected stochastic subgradient bound \cite{nemirovski2009}.
(ii) Given $B^t$, the integrand of \eqref{eq:psi}, averaged over $I^t$, is
jointly convex in $(\theta,\tau)$ and lines 5--7 compute one of its
subgradients, so the iteration is projected stochastic subgradient descent
on $\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}$ over $\mathcal{C}\times[0,M]$, of diameter
$\sqrt{D^2+M^2}$. As in \textsf{FedeRage}
\cite[Thm.~2]{rahimi2026federage}, $\omega^t\le\kappa W[\cdot,j]$ and the
$\tau$-step is at most $\kappa$, so the second moment grows to
$\kappa^2(G^2+1)$ and the rate by $\kappa$. Finally, $p\in\Uset(\hat p)$
gives $F_p\le F^{\alpha,\gamma}_{\hat p}$ by \eqref{eq:meancvar}, and
\eqref{eq:sandwich} at $(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)$ gives
\eqref{eq:certificate}.\hfill$\square$

Under \eqref{eq:hall}, part (i) matches the rate of SGD that sees every
group at every step, and the sampler affects the guarantee only through
feasibility. When \eqref{eq:hall} fails, part (ii) bounds the whole target
risk by the quantity the algorithm drives down. This bound costs a factor
$\kappa\ge\rho$ in the rate, which no normalized rule can lower, and a
premium $\min\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}-\min F_p\ge0$ that grows with $\kappa$.
Transport fixes the weighting under which the risk is averaged, and CVaR
hedges the part of the target that this weighting misses.


\noindent\textbf{When transport pays.} Theorem~\ref{thm:rate} measures
\avot{} against the optimum of its own weighting $\hat p$. To compare
rules at a finite horizon, let $\Phi(w)\triangleq F_p(\theta^\star_w)$,
with minimizers taken unique. Any rule with effective weighting $w$ then
satisfies\vspace{-8pt}

\begin{equation}
\E F_p(\bar\theta_T)-F_p(\theta^\star_p)
=\underbrace{\Phi(w)-\Phi(p)}_{\textnormal{\small alignment floor}}
+\underbrace{\E F_p(\bar\theta_T)-\Phi(w)}_{\textnormal{\small residual }\varepsilon_T(w)}.
\label{eq:infeasible}
\end{equation}
The alignment floor is nonnegative, independent of $T$ and at most
$M\|p-w\|_1$. The residual vanishes as $T\to\infty$, but it has no rate in
general and can be negative. Transport ($w=\hat p$) therefore beats
another rule ($w=\tilde p$) at horizon $T$ exactly when\vspace{-8pt}

\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{floor}}\triangleq\Phi(\tilde p)-\Phi(\hat p)\;>\;
\varepsilon_T(\hat p)-\varepsilon_T(\tilde p).
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}\vspace{-12pt}

The \emph{alignment gain} on the left depends only on the instance. The
right side depends on the stepsize and on the variance of each rule, and
transport can raise its own variance by putting large weights on rarely
observed groups. A weighting closer to $p$ can therefore still lose.
Condition \eqref{eq:gain}, and no distance between weightings, decides
the comparison.

\vspace{-16pt}\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}
% =====================================================================
\vspace{-8pt}
\noindent\textbf{Setup.} On Adult \cite{uci_adult}, logistic regression
predicts whether income exceeds \$50{,}000 (cross-entropy, $\eta=0.1$). We form $100$ single-race groups of $30$ records in the data's
race proportions, $85$ White, $10$ Black, $3$ Asian-Pac-Islander, $1$
Amer-Indian-Eskimo and $1$ Other, and the target weights the races
equally, so Other carries $p_i=0.2$. On IMDb-Wiki
\cite{rothe2018imdbwiki}, linear regression predicts age (MSE,
$\eta=10^{-2}$) from $128$ PCA features of a pretrained ResNet-18; $100$
identities with $30$ images each form the groups, and
$p_i\propto(101-i)^3$ puts $60\%$ of the target on the top $20$. Each step
draws $K=3$ groups without replacement with weights $\sigma_i$ of skew
$\beta$. On Adult, $\sigma_i\propto p_i^{\beta}$ runs from uniform
($\beta=0$, $\nu=0.60$; Other enters $3\%$ of the batches but carries
$20\%$ of the target) to aligned sampling ($\beta=1$, $\nu=0$). On
IMDb-Wiki, $\sigma_i\propto i^{\beta}$ increasingly favors the least
important identities, and $\sigma_i\propto101-i$ is aligned.
Each drawn group takes $H=5$ local full-batch steps ($H=1$ in
Theorem~\ref{thm:rate}), and all runs use $\eta_t=\eta/(1+t/1000)$ for
$4000$ steps and report $F_p$ over the last $500$ iterates and $5$ seeds,
with Welch's $t$ (clear if $|t|>3$). All rules see the
same batches: group-blind averaging ($1/K$ each, $\tilde p_i=r_i/K$),
upsampling ($\propto p_i/r_i$, renormalized), the unnormalized multiplier $(N/K)p_i$ \cite{rahimi2025fedavot}, LDS \cite{yang2021dir} on
IMDb-Wiki, which reweights by label rarity, and full access, an unrealizable
reference seeing every group at every step, weighted by
$p$. \avot{} uses $\gamma=0$ unless stated.

\noindent\textbf{Correcting the weighting lowers the target risk}
(Table~\ref{tab:results}a). Under uniform sampling, \avot{} closes $52\%$
of the gap between group-blind averaging and full access on Adult and
$64\%$ on IMDb-Wiki, and $61\%$ under aligned IMDb-Wiki sampling, all with $t\ge11$. Upsampling recovers most, not all, of this gain ($t=5.1$ on Adult, $2.3$ on IMDb-Wiki), and LDS, which ignores
availability, trails group-blind averaging. On aligned Adult there is nothing to correct: every normalized rule lies within $4\cdot10^{-4}$
of full access, whereas the fixed multiplier, whose batch weights sum to up to
$15.6$, reaches $0.670$ and $8504$ on uniform cells and diverges on aligned
ones.

\noindent\textbf{Transport wins while the alignment gain exceeds its noise
cost} (Table~\ref{tab:results}b). As $\beta$ grows,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{floor}}$ of \eqref{eq:gain} falls from $6.99$ to $0.02$,
and the measured gain trails it by $1.4$ to $2.3$, the price of noisier
weights. Transport wins for $\beta\le1$, ties at $\beta=1.5$
and $2$, and loses at $\beta=3$ ($t=-7.1$), although its weighting is
closer to $p$ at every skew. With a constant stepsize this price grows,
and transport loses at all six IMDb-Wiki skews, by up to $7.9$, while it wins at every
Adult skew.

\noindent\textbf{The groups that matter most benefit} (Fig.~\ref{fig:rare}).
On Adult, transport lowers the cross-entropy of race Other at every skew,
to $0.073$ against $0.119$ for group-blind averaging under uniform
sampling (full access: $0.031$). On IMDb-Wiki, the top $20$ identities, least observed once $\beta>0$, improve while $\nu\le0.59$, tie at $\nu=0.70$, and lose for $\nu\ge0.77$, where their weights
and noise peak.

\noindent\textbf{CVaR buys a guarantee, not a lower loss.} Over a
$10\times10$ grid of $(\alpha,\gamma)$, $F_p$ is lowest at, or within
noise of, $\gamma=0$ in every instance, the premium of
Theorem~\ref{thm:rate}(ii). The layer lowers the loss of the worst-off
race on uniform Adult to $0.3624$ at $(\alpha,\gamma)=(0.2,0.3)$, outside
the certified region, against $0.3679$ for \avot{} and $0.3994$ for
group-blind averaging, but never helps the least represented group, which
it can reweight only when a batch contains it. Code, curves and the full
grid are at
\href{https://github.com/vihaan-goyal/group-fair-sgd-ot}{\nolinkurl{github.com/vihaan-goyal/group-fair-sgd-ot}}.

%===========================================%==========================
\vspace{-10pt}\section{Conclusion}
\vspace{-5pt}% =====================================================================

Minibatch SGD, and every normalized rule that reweights the groups of a
batch, optimizes a weighting fixed by the sampler rather than the
declared importance, a bias that upsampling does not remove. Choosing the weights of all batch compositions
jointly by masked optimal transport yields \avot{}, unbiased under
coverage at the rate of full-access SGD and, beyond coverage, certified
by a mean--CVaR layer at the least possible price. Extending these
guarantees to nonconvex models remains open.

\vspace{-4pt}\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

Machine learning fits a model by minimizing its average loss over the
population it will serve, at scale by stochastic gradient descent (SGD),
one small random batch at a time \cite{nemirovski2009,bottou2018optimization}.
SGD thus follows the population loss in the proportions in which the data
arrive, and those proportions are rarely the ones that matter.
Populations consist of groups, such as demographic categories, speakers
or identities, whose share of the data differs from their importance:
face-analysis systems err most on the intersections least present in
their training data \cite{buolamwini2018gender}, speech recognizers make
markedly more errors for Black than for white speakers
\cite{koenecke2020speech}, and imbalanced regression errs most where labels
are scarce \cite{yang2021dir}. Imbalance is the norm
\cite{he2009imbalanced}, and SGD inherits it: each group steers the updates
in proportion to its frequency.

Fig.~\ref{fig:motivation} makes this concrete. Two groups, one nine times
as frequent as the other, follow different trends, and the learner asks
to weigh them equally. Uniform SGD fits the frequent group and leaves the
rare one with nearly twenty times its error ($1.74$ against $0.09$ MSE);
upsampling the rare group inside each batch narrows the gap to $0.95$
against $0.31$; \avot{} recovers the target line, with an MSE of $0.58$ on
both groups.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/motivation.pdf}
\vspace{-16pt}
\caption{Two groups ($900/100$ samples, trends $\pm2\sigma$ shaded),
i.i.d.\ batches of $b{=}7$, target $p=(\frac12,\frac12)$; legend, MSE on
the majority / minority group.}
\label{fig:motivation}
\vspace{-10pt}
\end{figure}

Existing remedies act on the sampler, the loss or the objective.
Resampling \cite{he2009imbalanced,chawla2002smote} and balanced sampling
\cite{idrissi2022simple} need control of the sampler, and down-sampling
wastes data. Reweighting by class \cite{cui2019classbalanced} or label
\cite{yang2021dir} frequency and margin or logit adjustment
\cite{cao2019ldam,menon2021logit} favor rare classes, though importance
weights fade on separable data \cite{byrd2019importance}, and importance
sampling \cite{needell2014sgd,katharopoulos2018not} only reduces variance.
Group distributionally robust optimization (DRO) \cite{sagawa2020dro} and
its label-free variants \cite{hashimoto2018repeated,liu2021jtt}, CVaR and
DRO objectives \cite{namkoong2017variance,levy2020large,curi2020adaptive},
and constrained methods
\cite{hardt2016equality,agarwal2018reductions,cotter2019twoplayer,chamon2020pacc,chamon2023nonconvex}
change the objective itself. In streaming, sharded or access-restricted
pipelines, however, batches arrive as they come, and the only lever left
is how the groups present in each batch are weighted.

Upsampling, the natural choice, sets each group's weight from its own
frequency and renormalizes the batch, so the weight the frequent group
collects when alone is never returned to the rare group. We instead
choose the weights of all batch compositions jointly, as a transport plan
from how often each combination of groups appears to how much each group
matters. This carries over \textsf{FedAVOT} \cite{rahimi2025fedavot} and
\textsf{FedeRage} \cite{rahimi2026federage}, which correct and hedge the
analogous mismatch between client participation and client importance in
federated learning. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt\parskip0pt\topsep2pt
\item \emph{What reweighting can reach.} SGD, and every normalized rule
that reweights the groups of a batch, optimizes a surrogate whose
weighting lies in a polytope fixed by the sampler; the target lies in it
exactly when a Hall-type coverage condition holds, checked by one
max-flow before training.
\item \emph{Exact alignment.} In feasible cases, \avot{} is unbiased for the
target and converges at the $O(1/\sqrt T)$ rate of SGD whatever the batch composition.
\item \emph{Certified robustness beyond coverage.} Otherwise, in infeasible cases, we show that Sinkhorn
returns the max--min fair Kullback--Leibler projection of the target onto the reachable set,
and a mean--CVaR layer certifies the target risk at the smallest price
any normalized rule admits. One theorem covers both regimes at the $O(1/\sqrt T)$ rate, inflated by $\kappa$ under CVaR.
\item \emph{Evidence.} On Adult and IMDb-Wiki, \avot{} closes
$52$--$64\%$ of the gap between group-blind averaging and full-access
SGD, and lowers the cross-entropy of race Other (under $1\%$ of the records)
from $0.119$ to $0.073$. Across sampler skews it wins while the risk
saved by correcting the weighting exceeds the extra optimization error
of its larger weights, and loses once that saving vanishes, although
its weighting stays closer to $p$ throughout.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{-6pt}\section{Problem Formulation}
\vspace{-8pt}
\label{sec:setup}
% =====================================================================

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Group-fair SGD by masked transport (\avot{})}
\label{alg:gavot}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $p$, $(q,\{A_j\},E)$, stepsizes $\eta_t$, horizon $T$,
$\alpha\in(0,1]$, $\gamma\in[0,1]$
\State $\Gamma\leftarrow$ Sinkhorn$(\mathbf 1_E;p,q)$,
$\ W\leftarrow\Gamma\,\mathrm{diag}(q)^{-1}$ \Comment{offline}
\State $\theta^0\in\mathcal{C}$, $\ \tau^0\in[0,M]$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \State draw $B^t$; find $j$ with $A_j=A^t$; get $g^t_i,\hat f^t_i$, $i\in A^t$
  \State $c^t_i\leftarrow\mathbf{1}\{\hat f^t_i\ge\tau^{t-1}\}$, $\ \omega^t_i\leftarrow W[i,j]\big(1-\gamma+\tfrac{\gamma}{\alpha}c^t_i\big)$
  \State $\theta^{t}\leftarrow\Proj\big(\theta^{t-1}-\eta_t\sum_{i\in A^t}\omega^t_i\,g^t_i\big)$
  \State $\tau^{t}\leftarrow\Pi_{[0,M]}\big(\tau^{t-1}-\eta_t\gamma\big(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i\in A^t}W[i,j]c^t_i\big)\big)$
\EndFor
\Ensure $(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\theta^t,\tau^t)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
Let $\mathcal{X}\subseteq\R^{d_x}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ be the input and
output spaces, and let $\Nn{N}$ index the groups over which fairness is
required. The training set $\mathcal{S}$ holds $n$ triples $(x,y,z)$ with
group label $z\in\Nn{N}$. Group $i$ has $n_i$ examples, empirical law
$\mathcal{D}_i$ and \emph{prevalence} $\pi_i\triangleq n_i/n$. A model
$m(\cdot\,;\theta)$ with parameter $\theta$ in a convex compact set
$\mathcal{C}\subseteq\R^{d}$ incurs a loss $\ell(m(x;\theta),y)\in[0,M]$,
and group $i$ incurs the risk
$f_i(\theta)\triangleq\E_{\mathcal{D}_i}[\ell(m(X;\theta),Y)]$. Each
weighting $w\in\simplex{N}$ of the groups defines the risk
$F_w\triangleq\sum_iw_if_i$ with a minimizer
$\theta^\star_w\in\argmin_{\mathcal{C}}F_w$. Three weightings matter in
what follows. The learner declares the first, the sampler imposes the
second, and a reweighting rule achieves the third.

\noindent\textbf{Target.} The learner declares a \emph{target}
(importance) distribution $p\in\simplex{N}$, which may be uniform,
inverse-prevalence, or set by a fairness specification
\cite{mohri2019agnostic,li2020fair}, and seeks
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\ F_p(\theta)=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_if_i(\theta).
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
\vspace{-10pt}

\noindent\textbf{Availability.} SGD never sees $p$. It sees random
batches, and the groups present in them determine which objectives it can
optimize. At step $t$ the batch $B^t$ holds $b$ examples drawn i.i.d.\
uniformly from $\mathcal{S}$, independently across steps. The groups it
contains form the \emph{active set} $A^t\triangleq\{z_k:k\in B^t\}$, which
takes values in a family $\{A_j\}_{j\in\Nn{J}}$ with law
$q_j\triangleq\PP[A^t=A_j]$, the \emph{availability distribution}. The
\emph{mask} $E\triangleq\{(i,j):i\in A_j\}$ records which groups each
composition contains. The \emph{coverage}
$\bar q(I)\triangleq\PP[A^t\cap I\neq\emptyset]$ of a set
$I\subseteq\Nn{N}$ is the probability that a batch contains at least one
of its groups, and group $i$ is observed at rate
$r_i\triangleq\bar q(\{i\})$. With $\pi(I)\triangleq\sum_{i\in I}\pi_i$,
inclusion--exclusion gives
\begin{multline}
\PP[A^t\!=\!A]=\textstyle\sum_{A'\subseteq A}(-1)^{|A\setminus A'|}\pi(A')^b,
\\ \bar q(I)=1-(1-\pi(I))^b,
\label{eq:iidlaw}
\end{multline}
so $r_i=1-(1-\pi_i)^b$. The analysis uses the sampler only through $q$,
so it covers every sampler with i.i.d.\ active sets, including the one of
Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}, which draws $K$ groups per step.

\noindent\textbf{Group-weighting rules.} Within a batch, the only freedom
left is how to weight the groups present. Let $g^t_i$ and $\hat f^t_i$ be
the subgradient and the loss at $\theta^{t-1}$, averaged over the
$n_i(B^t)$ examples of group $i$ in $B^t$. SGD and each of its
corrections step as $\theta^t=\Proj(\theta^{t-1}-\eta_t\hat g^t)$ along
$\hat g^t=\sum_{i\in A^t}\omega^t_ig^t_i$, and they differ only in the
batch weights $\omega^t$. A \emph{group-weighting rule} chooses
$\omega^t\in\simplex{N}$ supported on $A^t$ and depending on $B^t$ only
through its labels $\mathcal{Z}^t\triangleq(z_k)_{k\in B^t}$. We call such
rules \emph{normalized}, since the weights of every batch sum to one.
When the weights depend only on $A^t$, the rule is a matrix $W$ whose
$j$-th column weights the batches of composition $A_j$, which is the
client-weighting setting of \cite{rahimi2025fedavot,rahimi2026federage}
with groups in place of clients. Uniform SGD uses
$\omega^t_i=n_i(B^t)/b$, group-blind averaging $\omega^t_i=1/|A^t|$, and
upsampling $\omega^t_i\propto p_i/r_i$ on $A^t$. The \emph{effective
weighting} of a rule is $\tilde p\triangleq\E[\omega^t]$, and the next
result shows that it is the weighting the rule optimizes.
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{proposition}[Reachable objectives]
\label{prop:surrogate}
If the losses are convex in $\theta$, the batches are independent across
steps, and, given $\mathcal{Z}^t$, the examples of each group $i\in A^t$
are i.i.d.\ draws from $\mathcal{D}_i$ (as for i.i.d.\ batches), then a
group-weighting rule is a stochastic subgradient method on $F_{\tilde p}$,
and $\tilde p$ lies in the \emph{reachable set}
\begin{equation}
\Reach\triangleq\big\{\Gamma\mathbf 1_J:\ \Gamma\in\R_+^{N\times J},\
\mathbf 1_N^\top\Gamma=q^\top,\ \mathrm{supp}(\Gamma)\subseteq E\big\};
\label{eq:reach}
\end{equation}
conversely, every point of $\Reach$ is the effective weighting of some
rule. Uniform SGD has $\tilde p=\pi$, and group-blind averaging the
survival weights $\tilde p_i=\sum_{j:\,i\in A_j}q_j/|A_j|$ of
\cite{rahimi2025agnostic,rahimi2026federage}.
\end{proposition}
\vspace{-8pt}
\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} Given $\mathcal{Z}^t$, $\omega^t$ is fixed and
$\E[g^t_i\mid\theta^{t-1},\mathcal{Z}^t]=s_i\in\partial f_i(\theta^{t-1})$,
so $\E[\hat g^t\mid\theta^{t-1}]=\sum_i\tilde p_is_i\in\partial F_{\tilde
p}(\theta^{t-1})$. $\Gamma[i,j]\triangleq q_j\E[\omega^t_i\mid A^t=A_j]$
meets \eqref{eq:reach} with $\Gamma\mathbf 1_J=\tilde p$, and any such
$\Gamma$ is realized by $\omega^t=\Gamma[\cdot,j]/q_j$ on
$\{A^t=A_j\}$.\hfill$\square$

Proposition~\ref{prop:surrogate} has two consequences. The first concerns
SGD itself. Its group counts are multinomial with parameters $(b,\pi)$,
so $\tilde p=\pi$ and
\begin{equation}
\E\big[\hat g^t\mid\theta^{t-1}\big]\in\partial F_\pi(\theta^{t-1}).
\label{eq:introbias}
\end{equation}
The sampler thus imposes the prevalence $\pi$. SGD is a stochastic
subgradient method on $F_\pi$ and converges to $\theta^\star_\pi$, not to
$\theta^\star_p$, for every horizon and stepsize, just as agnostic
federated averaging converges to the participation-weighted optimum
\cite{rahimi2025agnostic,rahimi2026federage}. The second concerns every
normalized rule. Some rule optimizes \eqref{eq:target} if and only if
$p\in\Reach$, that is, if and only if the availability $q$ can be
transported onto the target $p$ along the mask $E$. \textsf{FedAVOT}
\cite{rahimi2025fedavot} removes the analogous mismatch in federated
averaging by masked optimal transport, and \textsf{FedeRage}
\cite{rahimi2026federage} hedges it by CVaR. Section~\ref{sec:gavot}
carries both tools over to SGD.

\vspace{-6pt}
\section{Group-Fair SGD by MOT and CVaR}
\vspace{-8pt}
\label{sec:gavot}\label{sec:cvar}% sec:cvar kept as an alias
% =====================================================================

By Proposition~\ref{prop:surrogate}, designing a normalized rule amounts
to choosing a plan $\Gamma$, and the rule then optimizes the row marginal
$\Gamma\mathbf 1_J$. \avot{} chooses the plan in two stages. Masked
transport selects the reachable weighting closest to $p$, which is $p$
itself when $p$ is reachable, and a mean--CVaR layer bounds the part of
the target that no plan reaches. One theorem covers both stages.

\noindent\textbf{Transport as reweighting.} A rule optimizes $F_p$ exactly
when its plan, whose entry $\Gamma[i,j]$ is the mass that composition
$A_j$ sends to group $i$, solves the \emph{masked optimal transport} (MOT)
problem of \textsf{FedAVOT} \cite{rahimi2025fedavot}, a transport problem
\cite{villani2009,peyre2019} without cost,
\begin{equation}
\Gamma\mathbf 1_J=p,\qquad \mathbf 1_N^\top\Gamma=q^\top,\qquad \Gamma\ge 0,
\qquad \mathrm{supp}(\Gamma)\subseteq E .
\label{eq:mot}
\end{equation}
The rule is then $\omega^t=W[\cdot,j(t)]$, where $A^t=A_{j(t)}$ and
$W\triangleq\Gamma\,\mathrm{diag}(q)^{-1}$. The column constraint makes the
weights of every batch sum to one, the mask assigns weight only to groups
present in the batch, and the row constraint makes the weight of group
$i$, averaged over batches, equal to $p_i$. In Fig.~\ref{fig:motivation},
a batch holds only the majority with probability $0.9^7\approx0.48$. The
plan gives the minority $0.96$ of every mixed batch and reaches
$(0.48+0.52\cdot0.04,\,0.52\cdot0.96)\approx p$. Upsampling gives it only
$0.66$ and reaches $(0.66,0.34)$, because it weights each group by its own
rate, so the weight the majority collects in batches without the minority
is never returned. A plan with the marginals of \eqref{eq:mot} exists only
if the batches cover the target well enough, and
Proposition~\ref{thm:feas} states this condition exactly.


\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{proposition}[Coverage, cf.\ {\cite[Thm.~1]{rahimi2025fedavot}}]
\label{thm:feas}
Problem \eqref{eq:mot} is feasible, i.e.\ $p\in\Reach$, if and only if
\vspace{-9pt}

\begin{equation}
\textstyle p(I)\triangleq\sum_{i\in I}p_i\;\le\;\bar q(I)
\qquad\forall\,I\subseteq\Nn{N}.
\label{eq:hall}
\end{equation}
%For i.i.d.\ batches, $I=\{i\}$ and $I=\Nn{N}\setminus\{i\}$ give
%$\pi_i^b\le p_i\le1-(1-\pi_i)^b$.
\end{proposition}
\vspace{-6pt}
\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} Max-flow/min-cut
\cite{hall1935,fordfulkerson1956} on $s\to i\to j\to t$ with capacities
$p_i$, $\infty$ on $E$, and $q_j$; the lower inequalities of
\cite[Thm.~1]{rahimi2025fedavot} are \eqref{eq:hall} for
$\Nn{N}\setminus I$.\hfill$\square$

Condition \eqref{eq:hall} requires that no set of groups carry more
importance than the probability that a batch contains one of them. Since
$\bar q(I)\to1$ as $b$ grows, infeasibility arises with small batches and
rare groups. Two quantities measure how far an instance is from
feasibility, the \emph{Hall ratio}
$\rho\triangleq\max_{I\neq\emptyset}p(I)/\bar q(I)\ge1$, which equals one
exactly on feasible instances, and the \emph{infeasible mass}
$\nu\triangleq\sum_{i:\,p_i>r_i}p_i$ of the groups whose importance
exceeds their observation rate.

\noindent\textbf{Sinkhorn and the KL projection.} \avot{} computes the
plan by Sinkhorn scaling of $\mathbf 1_E$ to the marginals $(p,q)$
\cite{sinkhorn1967,cuturi2013,peyre2019}, once and offline, at a cost of
$O(|E|)$ per sweep. Under \eqref{eq:hall}, the scaling converges to a
solution of \eqref{eq:mot}. When \eqref{eq:hall} fails, no solution
exists, yet the iterates taken after each column scaling still normalize
every batch, and their row marginals converge \cite{gietl2013ipfp}. The
next result identifies their limit.

\vspace{-8pt}
\begin{proposition}[Closest reachable weighting]
\label{prop:proj}
The row marginal $\hat p$ of the limit of the column-scaled Sinkhorn
iterates satisfies
(i) $\hat p=\argmin_{x\in\Reach}\KL(x\,\|\,p)$, the I-projection of $p$
onto the reachable set \cite{csiszar1975}, and each composition sends its
mass only to the groups it contains with the smallest ratio $\hat p_i/p_i$;
(ii) $\max_ip_i/\hat p_i=\min_{x\in\Reach}\max_ip_i/x_i=\rho$.
\end{proposition}
\vspace{-8pt}
\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} (i) The limit is identified in
\cite{gietl2013ipfp}, 
and the second claim is the optimality condition of the projection; it
involves $x$ only through $x_i/p_i$, so $\hat p$ also minimizes
$\KL(p\,\|\,x)$ and is the lexicographically optimal base of the
polymatroid $\bar q$ \cite{fujishige1980}. (ii) For $x\in\Reach$ and any
$I$, $\min_ix_i/p_i\le x(I)/p(I)\le\bar q(I)/p(I)$ by
Proposition~\ref{thm:feas}, so $\max_ip_i/x_i\ge\rho$. By (i), the groups
$I_0$ of smallest ratio receive all the mass of every composition
touching them, so $\hat p(I_0)=\bar q(I_0)$ and
$\max_ip_i/\hat p_i=p(I_0)/\bar q(I_0)\le\rho$.\hfill$\square$

Transport thus returns the reachable weighting closest to $p$ in
Kullback--Leibler divergence, in either direction. It is also max--min
fair, since no normalized rule gives the worst-served group a larger
fraction of its importance. Under \eqref{eq:hall} this weighting is $p$
itself, and transport alone optimizes the target.

\noindent\textbf{Controlling the unreachable part by CVaR.} When $\rho>1$,
the weighting $\hat p$ differs from $p$, and optimizing $F_{\hat p}$
controls the target only through
$F_p\le F_{\hat p}+\frac M2\|p-\hat p\|_1$. \textsf{FedeRage}
\cite{rahimi2026federage} hedges the same gap in federated averaging with
a mean--CVaR objective that is robust to weightings near the imposed one.
We center this objective at $\hat p$. For $w\in\simplex{N}$,
$\alpha\in(0,1]$, $\gamma\in[0,1]$ and
$\kappa\triangleq(1-\gamma)+\gamma/\alpha$ \cite{rahimi2026federage}, let
\vspace{-8pt}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\label{eq:meancvar}
F^{\alpha,\gamma}_w&\triangleq(1-\gamma)F_w+\gamma\,\CVaR^w_\alpha(f)
=\max\nolimits_{v\in\,\Uset(w)}F_v,\\
\Uset(w)&\triangleq\big\{v\in\simplex{N}:\ (1-\gamma)w\le v\le\kappa\,w\big\}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\CVaR^w_\alpha$ is taken over the group drawn from $w$. The
equality is the dual representation of CVaR \cite{shapiro2009lectures},
written in $v=(1-\gamma)w+\gamma u$ with $u\le w/\alpha$. Hence
$F^{\alpha,\gamma}_w$ bounds the risk of every weighting whose ratio to
$w$ lies in $[1-\gamma,\kappa]$, and in particular bounds $F_p$ whenever
$p\in\Uset(w)$.

To optimize $F^{\alpha,\gamma}_{\hat p}$ from batches, we place the risk
on the observed loss, as \textsf{FedeRage} does. Given $B^t$, let
$I^t\mid B^t\sim W[\cdot,j(t)]$ be a group of the batch and
$L\triangleq\hat f^t_{I^t}(\theta)$, so that $\E L=F_{\hat p}(\theta)$ and
$\E[L\mid I^t=i]=f_i(\theta)$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:gavot} never samples
$I^t$. Lines 5--7 average over it in closed form and take exact projected
stochastic subgradient steps on the jointly convex Rockafellar--Uryasev
form \cite{rockafellar2000cvar}
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}(\theta,\tau)\triangleq
\E\big[(1-\gamma)L+\gamma\tau+\tfrac{\gamma}{\alpha}(L-\tau)_+\big].
\label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
Jensen's inequality conditional on $I^t$ then gives, for all
$(\theta,\tau)$,
\begin{equation}
F_{\hat p}(\theta)\;\le\;F^{\alpha,\gamma}_{\hat p}(\theta)\;\le\;\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}(\theta,\tau),
\label{eq:sandwich}
\end{equation}
so the algorithm drives down an upper bound on the robust risk around
$\hat p$.

\noindent\textbf{Why transport first.} This bound has a price. A bound
$F_p\le F^{\alpha,\gamma}_w$ valid for every loss profile needs
$p\le\kappa w$, so $\kappa\ge\max_ip_i/w_i$, and $\kappa$ inflates the
rate in Theorem~\ref{thm:rate}. Hedging around the prevalence $\pi$ that
SGD imposes costs $\kappa\ge\max_ip_i/\pi_i$. Hedging around $\hat p$
costs only $\rho$, the least any normalized rule admits by
Proposition~\ref{prop:proj}(ii). Transport therefore makes the CVaR layer
as cheap as possible, and the next theorem quantifies both stages.
\vspace{-8pt}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of \avot{}]
\label{thm:rate}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ have diameter $D$, let each $\ell(m(x;\cdot),y)$ be
convex on $\mathcal{C}$, let the batches be i.i.d.\ across steps with
$\E[\|g^t_i\|^2\mid\theta^{t-1},\mathcal{Z}^t]\le G^2$, let $\Gamma$ be the
Sinkhorn plan of Proposition~\ref{prop:proj}, with row marginal $\hat p$,
and let $(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)$ be the output of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:gavot}.\\
(i) \emph{Transport.} If $\gamma=0$ and $\eta_t\equiv D/(G\sqrt{T})$, then
$\E[\hat g^t\mid\theta^{t-1}]\in\partial F_{\hat p}(\theta^{t-1})$ and
$\E[\|\hat g^t\|^2\mid\theta^{t-1}]\le G^2$, independently of $|A^t|$,
$N$, $b$ and $r$, and
\begin{equation}
\E F_{\hat p}(\bar\theta_T)-F_{\hat p}(\theta^\star_{\hat p})\;\le\;DG/\sqrt{T},
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
where $\hat p=p$, i.e.\ the target itself is optimized, whenever
\eqref{eq:hall} holds.\\
(ii) \emph{Transport and CVaR.} Let $\gamma\in(0,1]$, $\alpha\in(0,1]$,
$C_0\triangleq\sqrt{(D^2+M^2)(G^2+1)}$, and
$\eta_t\equiv\sqrt{D^2+M^2}/(\kappa\sqrt{(G^2+1)T})$. Then
\vspace{-12pt}
\begin{equation*}
\E\,\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)
-\min\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}\times[0,M]}\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}\;\le\;\kappa\,C_0/\sqrt T,
\end{equation*}\vspace{-8pt}
\vspace{-8pt}

and if moreover $p\in\Uset(\hat p)$, i.e.\ $\kappa\ge\rho$ and
$1-\gamma\le\min_ip_i/\hat p_i$, then\vspace{-8pt}

\begin{equation}
\E F_p(\bar\theta_T)\;\le\;\min\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}\times[0,M]}\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}
+\kappa\,C_0/\sqrt{T}.
\label{eq:certificate}
\end{equation}\vspace{-8pt}

\end{theorem}\vspace{-12pt}

\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} (i) Conditionally on $\theta^{t-1}$, the
columns of $W$ sum to one and the rows of $\Gamma$ to $\hat p$, so
$\E\hat g^t=\sum_jq_j\sum_{i\in A_j}W[i,j]\,s_i=\sum_i\hat p_is_i$ with
$s_i\in\partial f_i(\theta^{t-1})$, and Jensen's inequality over
$W[\cdot,j]$ bounds the second moment by $G^2$. This is the
sample-to-model argument of \textsf{FedAVOT}
\cite[Lemma~1, Thm.~3]{rahimi2025fedavot} with one local step, closed by
the projected stochastic subgradient bound \cite{nemirovski2009}.
(ii) Given $B^t$, the integrand of \eqref{eq:psi}, averaged over $I^t$, is
jointly convex in $(\theta,\tau)$ and lines 5--7 compute one of its
subgradients, so the iteration is projected stochastic subgradient descent
on $\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}$ over $\mathcal{C}\times[0,M]$, of diameter
$\sqrt{D^2+M^2}$. As in \textsf{FedeRage}
\cite[Thm.~2]{rahimi2026federage}, $\omega^t\le\kappa W[\cdot,j]$ and the
$\tau$-step is at most $\kappa$, so the second moment grows to
$\kappa^2(G^2+1)$ and the rate by $\kappa$. Finally, $p\in\Uset(\hat p)$
gives $F_p\le F^{\alpha,\gamma}_{\hat p}$ by \eqref{eq:meancvar}, and
\eqref{eq:sandwich} at $(\bar\theta_T,\bar\tau_T)$ gives
\eqref{eq:certificate}.\hfill$\square$

Under \eqref{eq:hall}, part (i) matches the rate of SGD that sees every
group at every step, and the sampler affects the guarantee only through
feasibility. When \eqref{eq:hall} fails, part (ii) bounds the whole target
risk by the quantity the algorithm drives down. This bound costs a factor
$\kappa\ge\rho$ in the rate, which no normalized rule can lower, and a
premium $\min\Psi_{\alpha,\gamma}-\min F_p\ge0$ that grows with $\kappa$.
Transport fixes the weighting under which the risk is averaged, and CVaR
hedges the part of the target that this weighting misses.


\noindent\textbf{When transport pays.} Theorem~\ref{thm:rate} measures
\avot{} against the optimum of its own weighting $\hat p$. To compare
rules at a finite horizon, let $\Phi(w)\triangleq F_p(\theta^\star_w)$,
with minimizers taken unique. Any rule with effective weighting $w$ then
satisfies\vspace{-8pt}

\begin{equation}
\E F_p(\bar\theta_T)-F_p(\theta^\star_p)
=\underbrace{\Phi(w)-\Phi(p)}_{\textnormal{\small alignment floor}}
+\underbrace{\E F_p(\bar\theta_T)-\Phi(w)}_{\textnormal{\small residual }\varepsilon_T(w)}.
\label{eq:infeasible}
\end{equation}
The alignment floor is nonnegative, independent of $T$ and at most
$M\|p-w\|_1$. The residual vanishes as $T\to\infty$, but it has no rate in
general and can be negative. Transport ($w=\hat p$) therefore beats
another rule ($w=\tilde p$) at horizon $T$ exactly when\vspace{-8pt}

\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{floor}}\triangleq\Phi(\tilde p)-\Phi(\hat p)\;>\;
\varepsilon_T(\hat p)-\varepsilon_T(\tilde p).
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}\vspace{-12pt}

The \emph{alignment gain} on the left depends only on the instance. The
right side depends on the stepsize and on the variance of each rule, and
transport can raise its own variance by putting large weights on rarely
observed groups. A weighting closer to $p$ can therefore still lose.
Condition \eqref{eq:gain}, and no distance between weightings, decides
the comparison.

\vspace{-16pt}\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}
% =====================================================================
\vspace{-8pt}
We test the three predictions of Section~\ref{sec:gavot} on two tasks, a
classification task in which the target asks for equal treatment of
races and a regression task in which a few identities carry most of the
target.

The Adult dataset \cite{uci_adult}, drawn from the 1994 US Census,
describes about $32{,}500$ people, and the task is to predict whether a
person earns more than \$50{,}000 a year from features such as age,
education, occupation and marital status. We train a logistic regression
model with the cross-entropy loss and stepsize $\eta=0.1$. The records
split into five races, White, Black, Asian-Pacific-Islander,
American-Indian-Eskimo and Other, in proportions of about $85{:}10{:}3{:}1{:}1$.
We form $100$ groups of $30$ people, each drawn from a single race, and
give each race a number of groups that follows these proportions, namely
$85$, $10$, $3$, $1$ and $1$. The target weights the five races equally,
so the single group of Other carries $p_i=0.2$, while each White group
carries $0.2/85$. When groups are sampled uniformly, however, Other enters
only about $3\%$ of the batches although it accounts for $20\%$ of the
objective, and this gap is what \avot{} has to close.

The IMDb-Wiki dataset \cite{rothe2018imdbwiki} contains over $500{,}000$
celebrity face images from IMDb and Wikipedia, and the task is to predict
a person's age from a face. We map each face to $128$ features with a
pretrained ResNet-18 followed by PCA and fit a linear regression with
stepsize $\eta=10^{-2}$ under the mean-squared error (MSE), since age is a
continuous quantity. The MSE is measured in years$^2$, so an MSE of $86$
corresponds to a typical error of about $9$ years. We keep $100$
identities with $30$ images each, one group per person, which gives the
same $100\times30$ layout as Adult and keeps the many runs cheap. The
target is steeply skewed so that a few people dominate: person $i$ has
importance $p_i\propto(101-i)^3$, and the $20$ most important people
carry about $60\%$ of it.

At each step the sampler draws $K=3$ groups without replacement with
weights $\sigma_i$, and $r_i$ denotes the resulting inclusion probability.
A skew parameter $\beta$ sets how far the sampler departs from the target,
which models data sources in which some groups appear far less often than
they matter, and the infeasible mass $\nu$ measures the resulting
shortfall. On Adult, $\sigma_i\propto p_i^{\beta}$ with
$\beta\in\{0,\frac14,\frac12,\frac34,1\}$ runs from uniform sampling
($\beta=0$, $\nu=0.60$), in which races appear in proportion to their
number of groups, to aligned sampling ($\beta=1$, $\nu=0$), in which every
race appears about equally often. On IMDb-Wiki,
$\sigma_i\propto i^{\beta}$ with $\beta\in\{0,\frac12,1,\frac32,2,3\}$
increasingly favors the least important identities, so $\nu$ grows from
$0.31$ to $0.88$, and an aligned sampler $\sigma_i\propto101-i$ gives
$\nu=0$. Each drawn group takes $H=5$ full-batch gradient steps on its
$30$ examples from $\theta^{t-1}$, and the rule's weights combine the
resulting updates ($H=1$ is the setting of Theorem~\ref{thm:rate}). All
methods use the stepsize $\eta_t=\eta/(1+t/1000)$, which halves by step
$1000$ and damps the extra noise that large weights on rare groups create.
Each run lasts $4000$ steps, and we repeat it over $5$ seeds and report
$F_p$ averaged over the last $500$ iterates, with Welch's $t$ to judge
differences ($|t|>3$ indicates a clear one).

We compare \avot{} with four rules that see exactly the same batches and
differ only in how they weight the groups a batch contains. Group-blind
averaging gives each of the $K=3$ drawn groups the weight $1/K$, whatever
its importance, so it optimizes how often each group is seen,
$\tilde p_i=r_i/K$, rather than the target. \avot{} ($\gamma=0$) instead
solves the transport problem \eqref{eq:mot}, whose table has one row per
group summing to $p$ and one column per batch composition summing to $q$;
Sinkhorn fills it by rescaling rows and columns in turn, and when the
target is out of reach its rows settle on the closest reachable weighting
$\hat p$ while every batch still sums to one. Upsampling multiplies each
drawn group's weight by $p_i/r_i$ and rescales the batch to sum to one.
The fixed multiplier $(N/K)p_i$ \cite{rahimi2025fedavot} never rescales,
so a batch's weights can sum to as much as $15.6$, which acts like a much
larger stepsize. On IMDb-Wiki we add label-distribution smoothing (LDS)
\cite{yang2021dir}, which ignores both the target and availability and
reweights each example by the rarity of its age label. Full access, a
reference the sampler cannot realize, sees every group at every step,
weighted by $p$.

The first question is whether correcting the weighting lowers the target
risk, and Table~\ref{tab:results}a shows that it does. On Adult under
uniform sampling, \avot{} reaches $0.2269$ against $0.2479$ for
group-blind averaging ($t=40$), closing $52\%$ of the gap to full access.
On IMDb-Wiki it closes $64\%$ of the gap under uniform sampling ($86.06$
against $91.63$, $t=18$) and $61\%$ under the aligned sampler ($84.28$
against $86.37$, $t=11$). Upsampling, which renormalizes batch by batch,
recovers most of this gain but not all ($t=5.1$ on Adult, $2.3$ on
IMDb-Wiki), and LDS, which ignores availability, trails group-blind
averaging. On aligned Adult there is nothing to correct, and every
normalized rule lies within $4\cdot10^{-4}$ of full access. The
unnormalized fixed multiplier, whose batch weights sum to as much as
$15.6$, reaches $0.670$ and $8504$ on the uniform cells and diverges on
the aligned ones.

The second question is when transport wins, and the IMDb-Wiki sweep of
Table~\ref{tab:results}b answers it. As $\beta$ grows, the alignment gain
$\Delta_{\mathrm{floor}}$ of \eqref{eq:gain}, the most that correcting the
weighting could save with perfect training, falls from $6.99$ to $0.02$,
and the measured gain stays below it by $1.4$ to $2.3$, the price of the
larger and noisier weights. Transport therefore wins while the alignment
gain clearly exceeds this price ($\beta\le1$), ties at $\beta=1.5$ and
$2$, and loses at $\beta=3$ ($t=-7.1$), although its weighting is closer
to $p$ at every skew, so distance to the target does not predict the
winner.

The third question is whether the groups the target cares about most
benefit, and Fig.~\ref{fig:rare} shows that they do while the sampler is
not too skewed. On Adult, transport lowers the cross-entropy of race
Other at every skew, to $0.073$ against $0.119$ for group-blind averaging
under uniform sampling (full access reaches $0.031$). On IMDb-Wiki, the
$20$ most important identities improve while $\nu\le0.59$ ($77.0$ against
$86.4$ at $\nu=0.31$), tie at $\nu=0.70$, and lose at the three most
skewed samplers, where transport puts the largest weights on them and the
added noise dominates.

Finally, the CVaR layer provides a guarantee rather than a lower loss.
Over a $10\times10$ grid of $(\alpha,\gamma)$, $F_p$ is lowest at, or
within noise of, $\gamma=0$, where the layer is off, in every instance,
which is the premium of Theorem~\ref{thm:rate}(ii). The layer lowers the
loss of the worst-off race on uniform Adult to $0.3624$ at
$(\alpha,\gamma)=(0.2,0.3)$, a point outside the certified region,
against $0.3679$ for \avot{} and $0.3994$ for group-blind averaging, but
it never helps the least represented group, since it can reweight only
the groups present in a batch.

With a constant stepsize, the price of the larger weights grows, and
transport loses at all six IMDb-Wiki skews, by up to $7.9$ in MSE,
whereas on Adult it wins at every skew under both stepsizes. The
per-skew curves, the constant-stepsize runs, the full grid and the code
are in the
repository.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/vihaan-goyal/group-fair-sgd-ot}{\texttt{github.com/vihaan-goyal/group-fair-sgd-ot}}}

%===========================================%==========================
\vspace{-16pt}\section{Conclusion}
\vspace{-10pt}% =====================================================================

Minibatch SGD, and every normalized rule that reweights the groups of a
batch, optimizes a weighting fixed by the sampler rather than the
declared importance, a bias that group-by-group corrections such as
upsampling do not remove. Choosing the weights of all batch compositions
jointly by masked optimal transport yields \avot{}, unbiased under
coverage at the rate of full-access SGD and, beyond coverage, certified
by a mean--CVaR layer at the least possible price. Extending these
guarantees to nonconvex models remains open.
